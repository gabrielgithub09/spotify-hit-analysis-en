What this means
  • Audio characteristics don't explain everything: no single audio feature strongly explains popularity (R² ≈ 0.06 on a linear regression); one can wonder whether artist fame or marketing play a bigger role, though this dataset cannot demonstrate it.
  • The sound of hits has changed: average energy declines steadily (76.6 → 62.8) while danceability climbs sharply from 2017 onward; chart pop has become more danceable and less "energetic" in the raw sense.
  • Dance pop dominates: 36% of charted tracks belong to this single genre, well ahead of pop (5.7%) and Atlanta hip hop (3.9%).
  • Energy and loudness are strongly linked (r = 0.71), which makes acoustic sense, but worth keeping in mind so as not to treat these two variables as independent signals in a model.
Extension: tested on a dataset 89x larger (89,740 tracks, hits + flops)
  • The initial hypothesis isn't confirmed, and that's informative: a much larger, unbiased dataset doesn't push R² up (0.028 on 89,740 tracks, versus 0.06 on the Top 100); the conclusion "audio alone doesn't explain popularity" is confirmed with far more robustness, not contradicted.
  • Telling a hit apart from the rest works much better than predicting a precise score: a "hit (popularity ≥ 70) or not" classifier reaches an AUC of 0.715 from audio features alone. Hits have a recognizable profile: less instrumental, less acoustic, louder, more danceable (see the chart below).
  • Explicit content (a real flag) is associated with higher popularity: 36.9 on average versus 32.9, this time confirmed with the actual variable rather than an unreliable proxy.
  • The hit-conversion rate by genre is the most actionable finding: hip-hop/r&b converts 10.5% of its tracks into hits versus 3.5% for electronic/dance (the highest-volume genre in the catalog); a reading that's impossible without data that includes flops.
